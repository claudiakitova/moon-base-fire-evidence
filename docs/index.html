Has anything burned in space in a Moon Base atmosphere?

NASA plans Moon Base and rover cabins at about 30% oxygen or more and 10.2 psi (0.694 atm) or less, so astronauts can step into 100%-oxygen suits without long pre-breathing. More oxygen makes materials burn more easily. This dashboard puts every test in NASA's Physical Sciences Informatics (PSI) combustion tables on one chart, ranks the 24 investigations by how much they tell us about fire safety in a spacecraft or a Moon Base, and summarizes each one. Every value links back to its source.

What the evidence says

    Earth vs. orbit: do ground tests predict flight?

    NASA selects spacecraft materials using tests on Earth. Only one PSI table compares the same samples on Earth (1 g) and in orbit: Saffire-II, burned in an uncrewed Cygnus cargo ship at about 22% O₂ and 20 cm/s airflow. Values are copied from the PSI table; the last column is our reading of them.

    SampleMaterialThicknessAirflowOn Earth (1 g)In orbitComparison

    This doesn't mean Earth tests are always the safe side. BASS-II's PSI record states the opposite concern: its hypothesis is that materials in microgravity, with adequate ventilation, "burn as well if not better" than on Earth, so NASA's upward-burning Earth test "may not be conservative for flammability in space." Saffire-II is one set of conditions, not the general rule.

    Other investigations set out to connect Earth and orbit results but publish only the flight side in their PSI tables: BASS and BASS-II (bridging NASA's ground flammability test, NASA-STD-6001 Test 1, to flight), Saffire-I and III, SAME (Earth conditions matched to flight mass loss), and two ground studies that model the difference: SPICE soot analysis and SAME smoke simulation.

        Method and data

        • Source: the official "Experimental table" CSV of each PSI investigation (plus the BASS/BASS-II as-run spreadsheets for oxygen, pressure, airflow and CO), and each investigation's PSI record. Values are copied or unit-converted (mmHg→atm, bar→atm, fraction→%), never estimated.
        • Interpretations are flagged in the data: BRE cells reading "3400%" and "2100%" are read as 34% and 21% (spreadsheet formatting); a FLEX pressure of 0 mmHg is treated as missing; "LOW O2" text is not a number. FLEX-2's PSI table lists a test envelope, not individual runs, so it is shown as an envelope.
        • Inverse flames (oxygen issues from the burner into a fuel atmosphere) are hidden by default: their oxygen value is not the cabin atmosphere.
        • Ranking: score = material (0–35: habitat solids 35, solid emulator 28, smoke from spacecraft materials 25, droplets 15, gas flames 10, dust 8; ground studies get 70% of what they analyze) + role (25 for prevention, detection or suppression; 8 for fundamentals) + atmosphere (0–25, computed from the tables) + evidence (0–15: test rows and publications in PSI). The weights are our judgment; ranking.csv has every component so anyone can re-weight.
        • Mars: the brief names no Mars habitat atmosphere; the Moon Base ranking is the closest match until one is defined.
        • Scope: PSI only. Every count on this page is about what PSI's combustion tables hold, not everything NASA has flown. Not in PSI yet: Saffire IV–VI and SoFIE (solids in high-oxygen, low-pressure air), and partial gravity (Moon 1/6 g; LUCI and FM2 are expected later, per the challenge brief).
        • How we used SI: the data-processing code, this page's code and the investigation summaries were written with Claude (Anthropic), an SI tool, working from NASA PSI records and tables. The team chose the questions, scope and framing and checked the outputs against the sources. Each summary names the PSI record fields it comes from; each test row names its PSI file and row.
        • Not a safety certification. This maps where evidence exists. It does not predict fires or qualify materials.
